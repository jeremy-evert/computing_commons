\section*{Source and Problem Statement} The following puzzle is from Puzzle Prime: \begin{center} \href{https://www.puzzleprime.com/puzzles/brain-teasers/deduction/king-octopus-and-his-servants/} {\textbf{King Octopus and His Servants}} \end{center} King Octopus has servants with 6, 7, or 8 legs. The servants with 7 legs always lie, and the servants with 6 or 8 legs always tell the truth. One day four of the king's servants had the following conversation: \begin{quote} ``We together have 28 legs,'' said the first octopus. ``We together have 27 legs,'' said the second octopus. ``We together have 26 legs,'' said the third octopus. ``We together have 25 legs,'' said the fourth octopus. \end{quote} \textbf{Question:} Which of the four servants told the truth? \medskip \noindent \textit{Source:} \href{https://www.puzzleprime.com/puzzles/brain-teasers/deduction/king-octopus-and-his-servants/} {Puzzle Prime, ``King Octopus and His Servants.''}

\section*{Rules / Assumptions} Before solving the puzzle, we translate its natural-language statements into a precise mathematical model. The following assumptions describe the model used in this solution. \begin{enumerate} \item There are exactly four servants in the conversation. \item Each servant has exactly one of the following numbers of legs: \[ 6,\qquad 7,\qquad \text{or}\qquad 8. \] \item When a servant says, ``We together have \(n\) legs,'' the word ``we'' refers to the same group of all four servants in the conversation. Therefore, every servant is making a claim about the same total number of legs. \item A servant with 7 legs always makes a false statement. \item A servant with 6 or 8 legs always makes a true statement. \item Each quoted sentence is treated as a complete mathematical statement. For example, ``We together have 28 legs'' means that the total is exactly 28, not approximately 28 and not at least 28. \item The servants are counted once each, and ``legs'' has its ordinary meaning. We do not introduce missing legs, artificial legs, unmentioned servants, or other information not present in the problem. \item The four statements refer to the same group at the same time. The total number of legs does not change during the conversation. \end{enumerate} Let \(x_i\) be the number of legs belonging to servant \(i\), where \[ x_i \in \{6,7,8\} \qquad\text{for } i\in\{1,2,3,4\}. \] Let the total number of legs be \[ T=x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4. \] The four servants are therefore asserting, in order, \[ T=28,\qquad T=27,\qquad T=26,\qquad T=25. \] For each servant \(i\), the truth value of that servant's statement is determined by the servant's number of legs: \[ x_i=7 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{servant \(i\)'s statement is false}, \] and \[ x_i\in\{6,8\} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{servant \(i\)'s statement is true}. \] These assumptions do not tell us in advance which servant is truthful. They only establish the rules that a proposed solution must satisfy.

\section*{Work} Let \(x_i\) be the number of legs belonging to servant \(i\), and let \[ T=x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4 \] be the total number of legs belonging to the four servants. The four statements are: \[ \begin{aligned} S_1 &: T=28,\\ S_2 &: T=27,\\ S_3 &: T=26,\\ S_4 &: T=25. \end{aligned} \] A servant with 7 legs lies. A servant with 6 or 8 legs tells the truth. \begin{claim} The second servant told the truth. The second servant has 6 legs, the other three servants each have 7 legs, and together the four servants have 27 legs. \end{claim} \begin{proof} We first show that at least one servant must be telling the truth. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that all four servants are lying. Every liar must have 7 legs, so all four servants would have 7 legs. Their total would therefore be \[ T=7+7+7+7=28. \] But the first servant claimed that \(T=28\). The first servant's statement would then be true, contradicting our assumption that all four servants were lying. Therefore, at least one servant tells the truth. Next, observe that the four servants claim four different totals: \[ 28,\qquad 27,\qquad 26,\qquad 25. \] The group has only one actual total \(T\), so no two of these different equalities can both be true. Therefore, at most one servant tells the truth. Since at least one servant tells the truth and at most one servant tells the truth, exactly one servant tells the truth. The other three servants lie. Each lying servant must have 7 legs, so the three liars contribute \[ 3\cdot 7=21 \] legs. The one truthful servant must have either 6 or 8 legs. If the truthful servant has 6 legs, then \[ T=21+6=27. \] If the truthful servant has 8 legs, then \[ T=21+8=29. \] A truthful servant's statement must equal the actual total. None of the four servants claimed that \(T=29\), but the second servant claimed that \[ T=27. \] Therefore, the truthful servant must have 6 legs, the actual total must be 27, and the truthful servant must be the second servant. Thus, \[ (x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(7,6,7,7), \] and the second servant is the only servant who told the truth. \end{proof}

\section*{Check Your Answer} Our proposed solution is \[ (x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(7,6,7,7). \] We will check this answer in three ways: \begin{enumerate} \item verify the arithmetic and every servant's statement directly; \item review the major steps of the logical proof; \item compare the result with an exhaustive computer search. \end{enumerate} \subsection*{Direct Verification} First, compute the total number of legs: \[ T=7+6+7+7=27. \] We now compare each servant's statement with the actual total and with the rule determined by that servant's number of legs. \[ \begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c} \text{Servant} & \text{Legs} & \text{Claim} & \text{Actual truth value} & \text{Required behavior} \\ \hline 1 & 7 & T=28 & \text{False} & \text{Must lie} \\ 2 & 6 & T=27 & \text{True} & \text{Must tell truth} \\ 3 & 7 & T=26 & \text{False} & \text{Must lie} \\ 4 & 7 & T=25 & \text{False} & \text{Must lie} \end{array} \] Every row is consistent with the rules: \begin{itemize} \item Servant 1 has 7 legs and makes a false statement. \item Servant 2 has 6 legs and makes a true statement. \item Servant 3 has 7 legs and makes a false statement. \item Servant 4 has 7 legs and makes a false statement. \end{itemize} Therefore, the assignment \[ (7,6,7,7) \] satisfies every condition in the puzzle. \subsection*{Verification of the Logical Proof} The proof relied on the following steps. \begin{enumerate} \item If all four servants were lying, they would all have 7 legs. \item Four servants with 7 legs would have \[ 4\cdot 7=28 \] legs in total. \item If the total were 28, then the first servant's statement would be true. This contradicts the assumption that all four servants were lying. \item Therefore, at least one servant tells the truth. \item The four servants claim four different totals: \[ 28,\qquad 27,\qquad 26,\qquad 25. \] Since all four statements refer to the same fixed total \(T\), no two of these statements can both be true. \item Therefore, at most one servant tells the truth. \item If at least one servant tells the truth and at most one servant tells the truth, then exactly one servant tells the truth. \item The three liars must each have 7 legs, contributing \[ 3\cdot 7=21 \] legs. \item The truthful servant must have either 6 or 8 legs. Therefore, the only possible totals are \[ 21+6=27 \] and \[ 21+8=29. \] \item No servant claimed that the total was 29. The second servant claimed that the total was 27. \item Therefore, the truthful servant has 6 legs, the total is 27, and the second servant is the unique truth teller. \end{enumerate} Each step follows from the puzzle's stated rules, ordinary arithmetic, or a previously established step. \subsection*{Verification by Exhaustive Search} A separate Python program checked every possible assignment of leg counts. Since each of the four servants may have 6, 7, or 8 legs, the program examined \[ 3^4=81 \] assignments. For each assignment, the program: \begin{enumerate} \item calculated the total number of legs; \item evaluated each of the four numerical statements; \item determined the truth value required by each servant's number of legs; \item rejected the assignment if any servant's actual statement disagreed with the required behavior. \end{enumerate} The search produced the following summary:
\begin{verbatim}

Solutions found:      1
Solution legs:        (7, 6, 7, 7)
Total legs:           27
Truthful servant(s):  [2]
\end{verbatim}
The full search results are stored in the accompanying files: \begin{itemize} \item \texttt{king\_octopus\_bruteforce\_results.txt} \item \texttt{king\_octopus\_bruteforce\_results.html} \end{itemize} The program that generates both reports is stored in: \begin{center} \texttt{king\_octopus\_bruteforce\_report\_generator.py} \end{center} The computer search agrees with the logical proof and finds no second solution. \subsection*{What the Computer Check Does and Does Not Establish} The exhaustive search establishes that exactly one of the 81 assignments in our mathematical model satisfies all of the rules encoded in the program. However, the computer cannot determine whether we translated the original English problem correctly. A programming error or an incorrect assumption could cause a program to search the wrong mathematical model perfectly. For that reason, the logical proof and the computer search serve different purposes: \begin{itemize} \item The logical proof explains why the answer is forced. \item The direct verification confirms that the proposed answer satisfies every rule. \item The exhaustive search confirms that no other assignment in the model also works. \end{itemize} All three methods agree. Therefore, our answer passes the check: \[ \boxed{\text{The second servant told the truth.}} \]

\section*{One-Sentence Summary} The puzzle's rules force the unique arrangement \[ (x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)=(7,6,7,7), \] so the four servants have 27 legs in total and the second servant is the only servant who told the truth.

\section*{Strongest Disagreement} The strongest objection is that the exhaustive computer search is only as trustworthy as the mathematical model encoded in the program. A program may inspect all 81 assignments correctly while still answering the wrong question if we incorrectly interpret words such as ``we,'' ``always,'' or ``together.'' This objection does not defeat the solution because the assumptions are stated explicitly, the logical proof does not depend on the Python program, and the proposed arrangement is verified directly against every condition in the puzzle. The logical proof, direct check, and exhaustive search all reach the same conclusion.
